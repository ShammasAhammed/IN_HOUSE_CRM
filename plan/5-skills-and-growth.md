# Skills and growth

Oct 14, 2026 · @ahammed shammas p

A continuous skill, growth and review system for Oppllo — skill requirements per position, learning assigned by HR and team heads, frequent scoring in numbers, and 360 review including team members reviewing their leaders. The aim is that every person always has a current plan to get better, and that the company is visibly the one supporting it.

## Why this is its own module

In the HR spec I put performance reviews under Later. That was right for a generic 10-person company and wrong for this one, because of a fact particular to agencies: the skills decay. What a performance marketer knew two years ago is partly obsolete now. A designer who does not learn new formats falls behind in a year. An agency that does not deliberately upgrade its people quietly becomes a worse agency while everyone stays busy.

So this is not an appraisal system. Appraisal looks backwards once a year and decides rewards. This looks forwards continuously and decides what each person learns next. Different purpose, different design.

It sits beside HR rather than inside it for three reasons. It is used by team heads daily, not only by HR. It reads heavily from Projects, which an HR module should not. And it will keep growing — frameworks, learning content, benchmarks — while HR stays stable.

Four things it must do:

1. **Define** what each position must be able to do, and at what level
2. **Assign** learning to close the gap, by HR or by the team head
3. **Measure** continuously, in numbers, from real work rather than opinion
4. **Review** from several directions, including team members reviewing their leader

The cultural goal matters as much as the mechanism. People should experience this as the company investing in them, not grading them. Every design decision in this document bends towards that, because a growth system that feels like surveillance produces careful behaviour instead of better work.

## The skill matrix

Every position gets a list of skills with a required level. A person gets a current level against each. The gap between the two is the growth plan — the system never has to invent what someone should learn, it reads it.

Five levels, deliberately plain:

| Level | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Learning — needs supervision |
| 2 | Can do it with checking |
| 3 | Works independently — the standard for the role |
| 4 | Handles difficult cases, fixes others' work |
| 5 | Sets how Oppllo does this, and teaches it |

Skills come in three groups, and every position has all three:

**Craft skills** — the actual job. Meta campaign structure, creative testing, audience and offer strategy, copywriting, video editing, motion, design systems, Google Ads, analytics and reporting, landing pages.

**Client skills** — the part that decides whether good work survives contact with a client. Writing an update, running a review call, explaining results and bad months, handling a complaint, saying no to scope creep.

**Leadership skills** — for anyone with people under them, including interns. Briefing clearly, giving feedback, reviewing work, developing someone, planning a week's load, making decisions without escalating everything.

An example, at the level of detail each position needs:

| Position | Skill | Required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Performance marketer | Campaign structure and budget control | 4 |
| Performance marketer | Creative testing and reading results | 4 |
| Performance marketer | Client reporting and the bad-month conversation | 3 |
| Performance marketer | Briefing a designer or editor | 3 |
| Video editor | Short-form editing and pacing | 4 |
| Video editor | Shooting basics on location | 3 |
| Video editor | Understanding a performance brief | 2 |
| Social media marketer | Content calendar planning | 4 |
| Social media marketer | Writing in the client's voice | 3 |
| Intern, any department | Oppllo way of working | 2 |

Who owns the matrix: HR maintains the structure; the team head for each department defines the skills and levels, because only they know the craft. Review the matrix twice a year — in this industry, skill lists go stale as fast as the skills do. When a new skill appears (a new ad format, a new tool), adding it to the matrix immediately shows who in the agency can already do it and who needs to learn it.

## Growth plans and assigned learning

Every person has a live growth plan: two or three skills being worked on right now, never more. A plan with ten goals is a plan nobody follows.

Each goal carries a target level, a deadline, what they will do to get there, and how it will be shown. The last part is the one that makes it real — evidence, not a feeling. Evidence can be a piece of work, a test campaign, a teaching session delivered to the team, or a client outcome.

**Assigning learning.** HR or the team head attaches learning to a goal:

- A course or video, with a completion check
- An internal SOP or recording from the training library
- A practice task created directly in Projects, so learning sits in the same list as real work
- Shadowing a specific person on a specific client
- A stretch assignment: a real client task one level above their current work, with review before it ships

The system tracks assigned, started, finished, and shown-in-work. Only the last one counts as the skill moving up a level.

**At onboarding.** A new joiner's position automatically generates their first growth plan from the matrix gap. Day one gives them a clear picture: here is what this role requires, here is where you are, here is what you will learn first, here is who is helping you. Few agencies in Kerala can show a new hire that, and it is a real recruitment advantage.

**For interns.** Same mechanism, shorter path: a fixed set of skills from level 1 to level 2 or 3 over the internship, drawn from the department's standard intern plan. Their certificate and final review then come from actual recorded progress rather than an impression. Each batch improves the plan, and the training library grows with it.

**For everyone else, continuously.** Growth plans are reviewed monthly in the one-to-one, not annually. One goal closed, one new goal opened. The rhythm matters more than the ambition: a person who moves one skill one level every quarter is transformed in two years.

**The company's side of the bargain.** For this to feel like support rather than pressure, Oppllo commits visibly to something in return — paid learning hours inside work time, a budget per person per year for courses, and senior people's time for teaching. Record those in the system too: hours of learning given, money spent per person, teaching sessions run. If the agency's side is never visible, people correctly read the whole thing as demands.

## Continuous scoring

A score per person, per position, updated continuously rather than at review time. It has four parts, and each part must come from a different kind of source — that is what stops it becoming one person's opinion dressed up as a number.

| Part | Weight | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delivery | 35% | Projects: tasks completed on time, rework rate, creatives approved first time, deadlines met |
| Skill level | 25% | The matrix: current levels against required levels for the position |
| Growth | 20% | This module: goals closed, learning finished and shown in work |
| People feedback | 20% | 360 reviews from the people they work with |

Weights change by position — that is the parameter-changing you described. A video editor's delivery weight is higher and their client-skills weight lower. A strategist is the reverse. A team head carries an extra element: the growth of their own team members, because developing people is the job, not an extra.

**Frequency.** Delivery and growth numbers update automatically as work happens. Skill levels change when evidence is accepted, a few times a year. The 360 feedback runs quarterly. So the score moves continuously, but the conversation about it happens monthly in the one-to-one.

**Rules that keep the numbers honest.** Each matters more than it looks:

- A score is a conversation starter, never an automatic consequence. No pay or promotion decision is triggered by the system.
- Trend beats level. Someone at 60 and rising is doing better than someone flat at 75. The dashboard shows direction, not just position.
- Compare a person to their own position's requirement, never to another person. Ranking colleagues against each other in a 10-person office destroys the team.
- Everyone sees their own full score and exactly how it was calculated. No hidden scoring.
- Anything that cannot be measured fairly stays out. A half-measured thing becomes a target and then a distortion.

**The trap to avoid.** Whatever you measure, people will optimise. If on-time completion is weighted heavily, tasks get marked done early and badly. The protection is rework rate and approval-first-time sitting in the same score, so gaming one lowers the other. Watch for this in the first two months and adjust the weights.

## 360 review

Four directions, each answering what only that direction can see:

| Direction | Who fills it | What it covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self | The person | Own view of each skill, what they want to learn next |
| Downward | Their team head | Craft quality, reliability, progress against the plan |
| Upward | Their team members | Leadership: briefing, feedback, availability, fairness, development |
| Sideways | People they work with across departments | Collaboration: responsiveness, clarity of handover, ease of working together |

Questions come from the person's position, so an editor is not asked about campaign strategy and a strategist is not asked about export settings. Each question scores 1 to 5 against a described behaviour, plus one open line. Keep it to eight to twelve questions — anything longer and people click through without reading.

**The upward review needs the most care.** This is the piece you named specifically, and it is also the piece most likely to fail in a small team. In a department of two or three, a leader can usually guess who said what. So:

- Upward results are shown only as a group when at least three people responded. Below three, the leader gets the written themes and no scores, prepared so individual voices are not identifiable.
- The leader's own leader sees the detail. The leader sees the summary. That gap is what makes honest answers possible.
- Upward scores are used to decide what that leader learns next, never to punish them. If the first upward round results in visible annoyance from a leader, the system is finished — people will give safe answers forever after.
- You go first. As owner, publish your own upward review results to the team and state what you will work on. Nothing else establishes that this is safe.

**Rhythm.** A full 360 quarterly is enough; monthly is too heavy for 10 people. Between rounds, light continuous signals do the work: a quick thumbs on a handover, a note after a client call. Those feed the people-feedback part of the score without anyone filling a form.

**For interns and new joiners**, run a short 360 at the end of the first month. It catches a bad fit or a missing introduction while there is still time to fix it.

## Dashboards

Four views of the same data, each answering a different question.

**The person's own page** — my skills against what my role requires, my two active goals with deadlines, learning assigned to me, my score with its parts shown openly, my trend over six months, and the feedback I have received. This is the page that decides whether the system feels supportive. It should open on what they are growing towards, not on a number.

**The team head's page** — their department as a grid: people down the side, the department's skills across the top, levels in the cells. Gaps are instantly visible. Plus whose goals are overdue, who has had no one-to-one in six weeks, and the upward feedback themes about their own leadership.

**HR's page** — this is the control room for the system you described, where HR designs, plans and reviews:

- Agency-wide skill map: which skills are strong, which are thin, which rest on one person only
- Coverage risk: skills where only one person is at level 3 or above — the biggest hidden risk in a small agency
- Who needs support now: scores falling, goals stalled, no learning assigned, no recent one-to-one
- Who is ready for more: consistently above requirement, ready for a bigger client, a leadership step or a pay conversation
- Review cycle status: who has and has not completed this quarter's 360
- Learning given: hours and money invested per person, so the company's side is visible

**The owner's page** — the agency as a whole. Capability trend over time, single-person dependencies, bench strength before taking the ninth or tenth client, and the few people who need your personal attention this month. Not names in a ranking; just where to spend your time.

The "who needs support" and "who is ready for more" lists are the two that justify the whole module. Everything else is bookkeeping; those two change decisions.

## Where AI helps, and where it must not decide

AI is genuinely useful here, but only on one side of a hard line.

**Good uses:**

- Drafting the skill matrix for a position from the job description and what that person actually does in Projects, for the team head to correct
- Summarising 360 written comments into themes, which also protects anonymity better than raw quotes
- Suggesting what someone should learn next, given their gap and what the agency is thin on
- Finding learning material and turning it into a practice task
- Preparing the one-to-one: what changed since last time, what is overdue, what to ask
- Writing the first draft of a growth plan, to be edited rather than accepted
- Noticing patterns across people: a skill the whole team is weak in, a leader whose team all report the same gap
- Watching for drift: someone whose delivery numbers have been falling for six weeks

**Never:**

- Setting a person's score or changing a skill level on its own
- Deciding who gets promoted, paid more, or let go
- Judging the quality of creative work from numbers
- Reading private messages to assess anyone

The rule is simple: AI prepares, people decide. Keep a record of which suggestions a human accepted or rejected — if the acceptance rate is low, the AI is not helping and the prompts need fixing.

## Build order and honest risks

This is the last thing to build, after Projects and HR, because it feeds on their data. Without delivery numbers from Projects the score has nothing real in it.

| Stage | What ships |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Skill matrix per position, current levels per person, the person's own page, department grid |
| 2 | Growth plans, assigned learning, evidence and level changes, monthly one-to-one notes |
| 3 | Continuous score from Projects data, trends, the four dashboards |
| 4 | Full 360 including upward review, AI assistance, coverage-risk and readiness lists |

Start stage 1 with one department only — your own, or the one with the most interns — and get the matrix right there before writing one for every position.

The risks worth naming before you start:

**It becomes a scoring system people hide from.** The commonest failure. Protections: open calculation, no automatic consequences, comparison to the role and not to colleagues, trend over level.

**The upward review is never honest.** Only solved by behaviour, not design. You publishing your own results first is the single strongest signal available.

**It is abandoned in month three.** Growth systems die quietly when reviews stop happening. Keep the ritual small enough to survive a busy month — one monthly one-to-one per person, nothing more.

**It measures activity instead of capability.** Courses finished is not skill gained. This is why evidence shown in real work is the only thing that moves a level.

**HR designs it alone and nobody owns it.** HR runs the system, but team heads must write their own department's matrix. A matrix handed down is a matrix nobody believes.
